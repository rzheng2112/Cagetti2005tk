\section{Reproduction Results and Discussion}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:comparing}}

We compare our fixed-price household solution and young-only distribution with the published benchmarks. Cagetti and De Nardi compute their moments in a stationary general equilibrium with young and old households, bequests, and market clearing.

\subsection{Convergence and Computation}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} shows the update norms for the baseline household problem. The baseline meets the stopping rule after 115 iterations. The final sum of the three update norms is $4.75\times 10^{-4}$, below the tolerance of $5.0\times 10^{-4}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{convergence}
  \caption{Convergence diagnostics for the fixed-price household problem.}
  \whenintegrated{\label{fig:convergence}}
\end{figure}

The enforcement constraint is active in the computation. In the baseline run, all entrepreneurial candidate points are feasible in the resource sense, but only about 12.9 percent of young entrepreneur candidates pass the repayment-versus-default check. For old entrepreneurs the enforcement pass rate is about 67.0 percent. These rates count candidate capital choices before the household compares occupations.

\subsection{Value Functions and Policy Functions}

The top-left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:values-policies-kstar} compares the young worker and entrepreneur values at high entrepreneurial ability and middle labor productivity. Where no capital choice satisfies both the resource and enforcement conditions, only wage work is feasible. Elsewhere, the household chooses the branch with the higher value.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{values_policies_kstar}
  \caption{Value functions, saving policies, occupational choice, and entrepreneurial investment. The young entrepreneur curve omits states with no feasible capital choice. In the bottom-right panel, yellow marks entrepreneurship and purple marks wage work. Bands meet halfway between adjacent asset points.}
  \whenintegrated{\label{fig:values-policies-kstar}}
\end{figure}

The top-right panel shows that old high-ability entrepreneurs prefer continuing entrepreneurship over retirement throughout the plotted asset grid. This matches the intuition of the original paper: once a high-ability entrepreneur has access to the business technology, continuing can be valuable because entrepreneurial returns are high relative to the safe return.

The middle panels show next-period asset policies for workers and retirees. Both policies are below the 45-degree line at high assets in the plotted range, so next-period assets are below current assets at those grid points. The bottom-left panel shows the entrepreneurial capital choice $k^\star$. The old entrepreneur reaches a high business scale more quickly, while the young entrepreneur's $k^\star$ rises with assets and then flattens near the upper part of the discrete $k$ grid. Assets relax the ability to operate a larger firm, but two numerical and economic limits matter: the enforcement constraint and the discrete capital-grid ceiling $k_{\max}=18$. In the baseline run, 14 of 56 young entrepreneurial grid states and 6 of 80 old entrepreneurial grid states sit at that ceiling, so capital choice is not determined by enforcement alone.

The bottom-right panel shows the young household's occupational choice at each asset and labor productivity state for high entrepreneurial ability. Higher labor productivity raises the value of wage work, while higher assets can make entrepreneurship feasible. At the lowest labor productivity, entrepreneurship is selected at 23 of the 80 asset points, compared with 4 of 80 at the highest productivity. These fractions give each grid point equal weight.

Our young-only distribution has a lower Gini and smaller top wealth shares than the paper's baseline. It excludes aging and inheritance flows and gives no formal bound on concentration in the full population.

\subsection{Stationary Distribution Diagnostic}

Figure~\ref{fig:mu-marginal-assets} shows mass near both ends of the asset grid in the young-only distribution. The right panel shows convergence of the distribution iteration for this kernel.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{mu_marginal_assets}
  \caption{Young-only asset distribution diagnostic from the notebook.}
  \whenintegrated{\label{fig:mu-marginal-assets}}
\end{figure}

The partial-equilibrium counterfactuals in the notebook should also be read cautiously. Some one-at-a-time changes, such as raising the interest rate to 7.5 percent or changing the pension share, converge and leave the middle-productivity young entrepreneurship region close to the baseline. Four scenarios remain nonconverged after 500 iterations with large residuals: $\tau=0.12$, $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}=0.55$, $\eta=0$, and $(\eta=0,\beta=0.88)$. We treat those rows only as numerical diagnostics, not as valid comparative-static solutions or as reproductions of the paper's Table~7 experiments. The $n_k=35$ experiment uses a uniform capital grid on $[10^{-5},k_{\max}]$, not a denser version of the baseline hybrid grid.
