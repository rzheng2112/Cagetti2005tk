\whenintegrated{\label{microeconomic-literature}}\par\section{Wealth, Entrepreneurship, and Borrowing Constraints}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:microlit}}

The original paper is motivated by a simple empirical fact: entrepreneurs are a small part of the population but hold a large share of wealth. These facts motivate the model's focus on the relationship between entrepreneurship and wealth accumulation. In particular, the model examines how business ownership, saving decisions, and borrowing constraints contribute to wealth concentration.

Cagetti and De Nardi classify entrepreneurs using information on self-employment, business ownership, and active management. Table~\ref{tab:entrepreneurs-population} reports their benchmark definitions. The narrowest definition, self-employed business owners, is closest to the model idea of an active entrepreneur because it excludes households that only passively own a business.

These patterns of wealth concentration raise questions about how entrepreneurship affects saving and wealth accumulation. \cite{Evans1989-ws} study entrepreneurial choice and find that liquidity constraints can limit both entry into entrepreneurship and business investment. \cite{Buera2006-wj} examines this relationship in a dynamic setting, showing how individuals with low initial wealth may need to accumulate savings before entering entrepreneurship. Saving behavior is also important after entry. \cite{Gentry2004-rj} find that entrepreneurs have higher saving rates and wealth-to-income ratios, reflecting the relationship between saving and business investment. \cite{Quadrini2000-mp} studies entrepreneurship in a dynamic general equilibrium model and shows how entrepreneurial saving and investment contribute to wealth concentration and mobility. Building on these connections between entrepreneurial choice, saving, and wealth accumulation, Cagetti and De Nardi (2006) develop a life-cycle model that incorporates borrowing constraints and intergenerational wealth transmission.

For this project, the borrowing-constraint literature matters mainly because it tells us what to reproduce theoretically. The enforcement constraint limits borrowing by requiring that repayment yield at least the value of default. A young entrepreneur who defaults returns to wage work, and an old entrepreneur who defaults retires. That is why the notebook gives special attention to the entrepreneur branch and to the comparison between the value of honoring debt and the value of default.
