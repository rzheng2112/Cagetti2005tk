\section{Introduction}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:intro}}
\setcounter{page}{0}\pagenumbering{arabic}

Cagetti and De Nardi (2006) study why wealth is so concentrated in the United States and why entrepreneurs are so important at the top of the wealth distribution. Their starting point is that entrepreneurs are a small share of households but own a large share of total wealth. A standard incomplete-markets saving model can explain precautionary saving, but it has difficulty producing the very thick upper tail of wealth. The original paper adds entrepreneurship, borrowing frictions, and voluntary bequests to address this problem.

The model is built around a household that chooses between two occupations. As a worker, the household earns labor income and saves at the risk-free rate. As an entrepreneur, the household operates a business technology, chooses business capital, and can potentially earn a higher return. The key friction is limited enforcement. An entrepreneur can borrow only if repaying the loan is better than defaulting, keeping a fraction of working capital, and returning to work as a wage earner. Because of this constraint, personal wealth matters as collateral. Richer entrepreneurs can run larger firms, and successful entrepreneurs have a strong incentive to keep saving.

The original paper calibrates this environment to match moments such as the capital-output ratio, the fraction of entrepreneurs, transition rates into and out of entrepreneurship, relative wealth of entrepreneurs, and the wealth Gini coefficient. Its main quantitative message is that entrepreneurs and borrowing constraints help explain wealth concentration. More restrictive borrowing constraints reduce top wealth shares but also reduce firm size, aggregate capital, and the number of entrepreneurs. The paper also emphasizes voluntary bequests: dynastic saving helps some high-ability descendants enter or continue entrepreneurship.

My reproduction paper has a narrower goal. I reproduce and analyze the theoretical household problem using the existing notebook in this repository. I do not reproduce the SCF data cleaning, the full calibration, the complete stationary general equilibrium, or all counterfactual tables. Instead, I focus on whether the notebook correctly organizes the worker and entrepreneur branches, implements the enforcement constraint, solves the fixed-price household problem, and produces diagnostic results that can be compared with the original paper's benchmark results.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:microlit} summarizes the motivation from the original paper and the related entrepreneurship literature. Section~\ref{sec:model} explains the model. Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} describes the computational decomposition used in the notebook. Section~\ref{sec:parameters} organizes the parameters. Section~\ref{sec:comparing} compares the reproduction outputs with the paper's benchmark results and discusses the figures. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
